# Why people click: research and agent instructions

Researched 23 October 2026. Apply this to the script writer's `VISUALS.md`, thumbnail makers and independent reviewers.
The aim is to attract the intended viewer with a promise the film delivers. These are hypotheses to test on this
channel, not a guaranteed formula or a claim that psychology can predict an individual viewer.

## Findings and evidence

1. **Make the missing information clear and worth knowing.** Qiu and Golman (2024),
   [Curiosity in news consumption](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.4195), analysed over 100,000 WeChat news articles and
   validated their measures in five preregistered experiments. Salient information gaps, perceived importance and
   surprising topics predicted consumption. The field relationships are observational, not randomized evidence
   that a particular wording causes more YouTube clicks. Application: name a recognizable subject and leave one
   specific question unresolved, such as why the same device performs differently. Avoid empty "you won't believe this" teasers.
2. **Reduce the effort needed to understand the offer.** Shulman, Markowitz and Rogers (2024),
   [Reading dies in complexity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11152133/), report over 30,000 field
   experiments across The Washington Post and Upworthy, plus follow-up studies. Readers preferred simpler headlines.
   Application: common words, concrete nouns and one clear idea. This supports readable writing, not a universal
   character count or a rule that the shortest title always wins.
3. **Real problems can attract attention; alarm is not mandatory.** Robertson et al. (2023),
   [Negativity drives online news consumption](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4), analysed 22,743 randomized
   headline experiments with about 105,000 variants and more than 370 million impressions. For an average-length
   headline, an additional negative word increased CTR by about 2.3% relative, not 2.3 percentage points. These were
   Upworthy news experiments, not this channel. Application: use a warning when the film establishes a meaningful
   cost, risk or mistake; otherwise test discovery, usefulness, surprise or a supported result. Never manufacture fear.
4. **Thumbnail and title jointly explain the offer.** YouTube's
   [thumbnail and title guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en) recommends accuracy,
   succinct titles with important words first, readable type, simple composition and audience-specific choices.
   Application: one dominant subject and short text that adds a consequence, contrast or question to the title.
   A style reference such as the Kai sample is visual inspiration, not evidence of causal performance.
5. **A click is only the beginning.** The curiosity study did not equate increased consumption with sustained
   engagement. YouTube's [A/B testing guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en) selects
   winners using watch time rather than CTR alone. Application: raise the promised question early and answer it
   with evidence; inspect retention alongside CTR. Compare like traffic sources and audiences. Prefer concurrent
   randomized tests where eligible; before/after changes are confounded by time and audience. No clear winner is
   a valid result. Editorial rankings are predictions, never measured CTR results.
6. **The description supports discovery and expectation.** YouTube's
   [description guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449?hl=en) recommends unique descriptions,
   useful first lines and one or two main topic terms. Application: put the subject and viewer benefit in the first
   sentence, then scope or evidence in the second. Do not assume the description is visible on every discovery
   surface or has the same influence on initial clicks as the title and thumbnail.

## Required thinking and output

Before writing copy, identify the intended viewer, what they care about, the film's strongest supported finding,
one unresolved question, and the exact passage that pays off the promise. Work from the film and its research;
do not invent a more dramatic story.

**Channel preference: thumbnail text creates the mystery; the title establishes the subject; the video delivers
the answer.** Make this the default for every proposed package. Tease a specific incident, contradiction, consequence
or unexplained result without revealing the cause, solution or final verdict. Keep enough context in the title and
image for viewers to understand what they are curious about. A question mark is optional; mystery can be a statement.
For example, a PHP title can establish the security topic while thumbnail text such as "BEFORE YOUR CODE RUNS…"
leaves how that happens unresolved, rather than giving the lesson as "YOUR PHP EXPOSED". Use examples only when
the film supports them. "THE BIGGEST HACK OF THE DECADE" requires evidence for that comparison; dramatic scale
must not be invented to create mystery. This is the creator's editorial preference, not a research-proven optimum.
An explicit request for different wording takes priority. Descriptions explain scope and benefit without giving
away the central explanation or resolution.

The script writer develops **three genuinely different matched packages** in `VISUALS.md` under
`## Packaging alternatives`, then recommends one in the existing `## Thumbnail` section. Use distinct motivations
where the material allows: useful outcome, surprising explanation, and an evidenced problem or comparison.
If the brief pins a title or wording, preserve it and label other suggestions as proposals requiring the person's choice.

Every package contains:

- **Title:** specific subject plus a compelling benefit, contrast or unanswered question. Prefer plain words and
  put identifying words early. Around 45–65 characters is a drafting target, not a limit or proven optimum.
- **Headline:** usually 2–4 readable words on the thumbnail that create a specific mystery. Tease the stakes or an
  unexplained result; withhold the cause, solution and final verdict. Add to the title rather than copying it.
  Preserve qualifications that change meaning.
- **Description:** two sentences, roughly 30–60 words, with the topic and benefit first, followed by what the film
  actually explains or demonstrates. Use natural topic terms; no keyword stuffing, invented proof or generic hype.
- **Rationale:** intended viewer, why they would care, what they would want to learn, and what the image adds.
- **Payoff and support:** the scene/passage and research supporting the promise, including conditions on numbers.

These lengths are house drafting defaults; clarity and the person's brief take priority. Do not pad to reach a count.
The selected `## Thumbnail` includes `Title`, `Headline`, `Description`, `Audience`, `Click motivation`, and its usual
subject, design, relationship, payoff and sourcing fields. Packaging alternatives are recommendations, not approval.

Each thumbnail maker writes one coherent package into its variant's `BRIEF.md` with explicit `Title:`, `Headline:`
and `Description:` lines, plus rationale and payoff evidence. Preserve an explicitly approved title; propose changes
separately. Reply with all three pieces of copy so they can be reviewed together.

Thumbnail dimensions match the video's exact saved width and height for every personality and every variant. For a vertical video use a
9:16 portrait canvas, 1080×1920, with a 180×320 feed preview. For a landscape video use a 16:9 canvas,
1920×1080, with a 320×180 feed preview. Read width and height from the saved job; use the saved format for legacy jobs without dimensions. Square videos use 1080×1080 with 320×320 previews; custom sizes retain their aspect ratio in a preview whose longest edge is 320 pixels.
State orientation, output dimensions and preview dimensions in the visual plan's thumbnail section and every
maker brief. Compose the subject and headline for that frame; do not stretch, letterbox or simply crop a finished
landscape layout into portrait. Keep key text and subjects inside the video's applicable safe area and check
readability at the matching feed size. Chosen and archived files must retain the approved orientation.
Wrong orientation, clipping or distortion blocks thumbnail approval regardless of visual quality.

Reviewers assess immediate clarity, audience relevance, a worthwhile unanswered question, readability at the matching feed size,
complementary title/image copy, and whether both description and headline honestly match the film. Unsupported
claims block; a positive angle or question does not. Rank by expected appeal and delivery of the promise, explain
tradeoffs, and never claim a package is empirically the best without channel test evidence.
Check that a viewer can name the unanswered question after reading the title and thumbnail together. If the copy
gives away the central answer or is too vague to understand, request a revision unless the person explicitly asked
for that wording. Apply the same spoiler check to the short description.

After selection, the orchestrator presents the selected title and description for the person's decision. Use the
approved copy for upload (`youtube-upload --title ... --description ...`). Thumbnail selection alone does not imply
approval of a changed title. Follow the existing release and approval workflow; do not publish or start production
merely because packaging was proposed.
