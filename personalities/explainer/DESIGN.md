# Explainer design

## What makes the picture work (guidance, whatever the theme)
Break a guideline only when that makes the film better, and say why in the shot's "notes".
- Show the real thing, drawn. When the narration names something concrete (a product, company, device, place,
  website), show it so it is recognised in under a second without its label: an SVG drawing with its true silhouette
  and identifying details, or the company's SVG logo. No photos. Anonymous rectangles with labels are a last resort.
  Abstract ideas become familiar physical metaphors (a container that fills for memory, a hanging tag for a price, a
  stamp for a verdict, a strike-through for "no longer", a search box being typed into for "look it up") and the
  metaphor acts.
- Use the whole frame, one hero per beat. Spread the composition: main subject on one side, the hero number across
  from it, the evidence card in a free corner; content spans most of the frame, not the middle third. Each beat has
  ONE element that clearly dominates: a "hero" number or word (140–220 px, accent colour, small unit under it) or the
  main subject at a quarter to half of the frame width. Everything else is small and quiet: short uppercase labels
  ≥ 28 px, values ≥ 40 px. Dark empty background between elements is fine; a small centred cluster is not. Readable
  at 480 px wide. On-screen text is prices, names, labels, verdicts, never the narrated sentence (a quoted source on a
  card is the exception).
- Show the evidence. When the narration rests on what a source says (a post, article, paper, README, spec, benchmark),
  put it on screen: a "card" with the exact sentence retyped, the key phrase highlighted and a source chip, or a
  "sheet" with the spec's rows. No empty boxes (a rectangle holds a value, drawing or quote) and no washed-out frames
  where everything is half-faded.
- One stage per idea, built up in front of the viewer: the container appears, it fills, a tag drops onto it, the
  verdict stamps. A stage can live 20–40 s while it builds; pan the camera to what is new. When the idea changes,
  clear it, transform it or cut cleanly instead of zooming out to fit more.
- Dynamic: every spoken beat lands as a visible event at its words (arrive, drop, stamp, strike, count, grow, swap,
  transform), one clear event at a time in quick succession. Holds are short (a payoff registers for 1–2 s, then the
  picture moves on). Nothing appears before it is said, and nothing simply appears: every object arrives with motion
  (draws on, drops, pops, slides, types, grows, transforms). Things the narration describes being set up assemble
  part by part on their cues, in the theme's motion language; do not start a scene with them already on screen.
- Motion has one personality, set in the theme (theme.eases) and used everywhere: drops that land, pops that settle,
  swings, stamps, crisp slides, or smooth precise moves. A fade alone says nothing about what happened.
- Explain, do not decorate: each important idea has a visible cause → consequence → payoff. Comparisons stand side
  by side under the same conditions and the difference is visible at a glance. If the same animation would explain an
  unrelated topic by changing labels, it is too generic. Do not explain later passages early.
- Continuity: recurring subjects keep their identity across scenes ("carry"); every transition has a handoff or a
  deliberate clean break.
