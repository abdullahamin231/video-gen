Create original uncanny fiction around a familiar situation. Use rules, violations and consequences to
build tension, choosing their number and order to serve the story. Keep geography, recurring subjects
and causal logic intelligible. Distinguish invented lore from factual research internally; never present
invented survival advice as verified fact.

Create new projects with `--profile rules-story`. The shared transport contract is `docs/orchestrator.md`.

Titles follow one shape: a concrete trigger in sentence case ("If your [object, device, app, person, place or
event] ever [specific anomaly]…"), an ellipsis, then a short instruction in capitals that tells the viewer to
do the opposite of what seems sensible. The trigger can be anything a viewer could plausibly meet in ordinary
life, online or in the story's world; it need not be domestic. Make the anomaly specific enough to picture,
and let the instruction imply the rule and its consequence without naming them. This shape governs the
title only. It may suggest a premise, but it does not constrain the story: you keep
full creative authority over the premise, structure, imagery, tone and ending, and should invent the most
effective original scary story you can. Examples: "If Your Kettle Ever Boils At 4:00… DON'T POUR IT"; "If
Your Phone Ever Types A Message You Didn't Write… DON'T SEND IT"; "If The Bus Driver Ever Skips Your Stop
And Says Your Name… STAY ON BOARD". Do not reuse a premise, object or instruction from an existing video.

Visual production defaults for horror videos:

- Open on a compelling, populated frame with the main subject and relevant props
  immediately visible; do not spend the opening revealing an empty room.

- Establish a dark, gloomy, unnerving atmosphere through restrained lighting, deep shadows and muted
  colours while keeping characters, interactions and story clues readable. Make figures large enough
  to read on a phone, filling the frame purposefully with characters, props and coherent environmental
  details. Avoid large unused areas, tiny subjects and arbitrary decorative clutter. Choose pacing,
  imagery, sound and deliberate stillness to sustain unease.

- Build a visual beat plan against the measured narration before authoring. Show concrete events as
  they happen: introduce people when mentioned, stage conversations through gestures and interactions,
  and depict meaningful time cues such as a clock reaching midnight. Animate subjects performing the
  narrated actions with readable intention, contact, action, reaction and consequence. Camera movement,
  transitions and typography support the action; they cannot turn a static illustration into an
  animated scene.

- Maintain a coherent visual world and consistent craft throughout. Preserve recurring subjects'
  identities, proportions and level of detail, and draw reusable characters and props at consistent,
  reasonable scales. Human figures have exactly two arms and two legs with proportionate limb lengths.
  Gestures replace the relevant limb pose: use one articulated limb that changes pose rather than
  overlapping separate resting and acting arms. References may guide tone and visual language without
  dictating the plot, a shot template or copied assets.

- Make spatial relationships and physical interactions convincing, including in stylised treatments.
  Hands grip tool handles, arms follow tools and feet meet the floor. Maintain contact, plausible reach
  and joint lengths throughout each movement, from initial contact through release.

- Apply the full [Motion Explainer reference](../../docs/motion-explainer.md) during visual planning,
  including camera, editing and motion graphics across the whole film. Read matching upstream examples
  and adapt their actual implementation. Wire adopted bridge or drawing code into the seekable scene
  timeline; installing the library or naming a technique is insufficient. Choose and combine techniques
  for this treatment without restricting the available vocabulary.

- Use smooth, purposeful camera movement that follows the story and preserves position and scale
  continuity between moves. Avoid abrupt viewBox resets, unexplained jump cuts and moves that crop out
  narrated action. Reserve deliberate cuts for a clear storytelling purpose.

- During revisions, preserve approved narration and successful visual details unless the requested
  change requires altering them. Correct defects in existing figures and animation rather than
  layering duplicate body parts or props over them.
